Paso 2 de 8

Fíjate Notice

Read how it works. Play the examples and repeat them aloud.

Plans: pensar + infinitivo

pienso · piensas · piensa · pensamos · piensan + infinitivo

Pensar (e → ie) means "to think", but pensar + infinitivo means "to plan to, to be thinking of": pienso caminar mucho. It's an Andamio structure. Ask about plans with ¿Qué piensas hacer…? Like preferir, the nosotros form doesn't change: pensamos.

From the conversation:

  • Bueno, ya tenemos el hotel y los boletos. Ahora, ¿qué piensas hacer en Guanajuato?OK, we've got the hotel and the tickets. Now, what are you planning to do in Guanajuato?
  • Pienso caminar mucho y verlo todo. ¡Es mi primer Año Nuevo en México!I'm planning to walk a lot and see everything. It's my first New Year's in Mexico!
  • ¿Y qué piensan hacer allá?And what are you planning to do there?
  • Pensamos ir a una callejoneada y subir al Pípila. ¿Qué me recomienda?We're planning to go on a callejoneada and go up to the Pípila. What do you recommend?

The future: cenaremos, llegará, iremos

infinitive + é · ás · á · emos · án

The future (el futuro) is easy to build: keep the whole infinitive and add the endings. The endings are the same for -ar, -er and -ir verbs, and all except nosotros have an accent. Use it for plans, predictions (hará sol) and promises (haré la reservación).

cenarcomerir
yocenarécomeréiré
túcenaráscomerásirás
él, ella, ustedcenarácomeráirá
nosotroscenaremoscomeremosiremos
ellos, ustedescenaráncomeránirán

From the conversation:

  • Diego cenará con sus papás en Puebla y llegará el viernes en la tarde. Tendrá que tomar un camión muy temprano, ¡pobre!Diego will have dinner with his parents in Puebla and get there on Friday afternoon. He'll have to take a really early bus, poor guy!
  • Cenaremos en el centro y a las doce comeremos las doce uvas, como manda la tradición. Después, ¡a la fiesta!We'll have dinner downtown and at midnight we'll eat the twelve grapes, as tradition says. Then, party time!
  • ¡Claro! Le traeré charamuscas.Of course! I'll bring you charamuscas.

Don't forget the accent: cenará, iré, llegarán. Without it, the word is misspelled or becomes a different form.

Four irregular futures: tener, hacer, poder, salir

A few verbs change their stem, but the endings stay the same. Learn these four now: tendr- (tener), har- (hacer), podr- (poder), saldr- (salir). Hará sol (it'll be sunny) is the future of hace sol.

infinitivestemyonosotros
tenertendr-tendrétendremos
hacerhar-haréharemos
poderpodr-podrépodremos
salirsaldr-saldrésaldremos

From the conversation:

  • Mira el mapa. Saldremos el jueves treinta y uno a las ocho de la Terminal del Norte y llegaremos a la una, más o menos.Look at the map. We'll leave on Thursday the thirty-first at eight from the North Terminal and we'll get there at one, more or less.
  • ¿Y qué haremos el jueves en la noche?And what will we do on Thursday night?
  • ¡Qué padre! ¿Y el viernes? Aquí en la laptop dice que hará sol y veintidós grados.Cool! And on Friday? Here on the laptop it says it'll be sunny and twenty-two degrees.

If…: si + present, future

si + present, future

To say what will happen if something happens, put the present after si (if) and the future (or ir a + infinitive) in the other half: si hace buen tiempo, subiremos al Pípila. The two halves can swap places: subiremos al Pípila si hace buen tiempo.

From the conversation:

  • Perfecto. Si hace buen tiempo, subiremos al Pípila: desde ahí se ve toda la ciudad. Y en la noche podremos ir a una callejoneada.Perfect. If the weather's good, we'll go up to the Pípila: from there you can see the whole city. And at night we'll be able to go on a callejoneada.
  • En enero casi nunca llueve allá. Pero si llueve, iremos al Museo de las Momias.In January it hardly ever rains there. But if it rains, we'll go to the Mummy Museum.

When si means "if" (a condition), never use the future right after it: si llueve, not si lloverá.

Three ways to talk about the future

Ir a + infinitivo (unit 8) is the most common in everyday Mexican Spanish: voy a buscar un restaurante. The future sounds a little more planned or certain, and it's typical for promises (haré la reservación) and predictions (hará sol). The present with a time word also works for fixed plans: el lunes trabajo.

From the conversation:

  • Muy bien. Yo voy a buscar un restaurante para la cena del jueves. Haré la reservación hoy en la noche.Great. I'm going to look for a restaurant for Thursday's dinner. I'll make the reservation tonight.
  • Sí, rápido: en Año Nuevo todo se llena. Ah, y el domingo saldremos a las cuatro, porque el lunes trabajo.Yes, quickly: at New Year everything fills up. Oh, and on Sunday we'll leave at four, because I work on Monday.

Cultura Culture

Read about the culture behind the language.

Puentes, vacations and the Guelaguetza

Since a 2006 reform of the Federal Labor Law, three of Mexico's official rest days always fall on a Monday: the first Monday of February (Constitution Day), the third Monday of March (Benito Juárez's birthday) and the third Monday of November (the Revolution). The result is a puente (literally "bridge"): a three-day weekend, when bus terminals fill up and roads to Acapulco, Puebla or Querétaro are packed. In 2026 the Revolution puente was Saturday 14 to Monday 16 November. When 25 December or 1 January falls on a Friday, as both do in 2026–27, there is a puente too.

The big vacation periods follow the school calendar of the SEP (the Ministry of Education). Semana Santa (Holy Week; in 2026, from Palm Sunday, 29 March, to Easter Sunday, 5 April) is the busiest week at the beach; schools usually close for about two weeks. Summer vacation runs roughly from mid-July to late August, and the December holidays from about mid-December to early January, when workers also receive their aguinaldo, a Christmas bonus of at least 15 days' pay. By law, since 2023 workers get at least 12 days of paid vacation after their first year.

In July, Oaxaca celebrates the Guelaguetza, from a Zapotec word for a gift given to the community. Dance groups from the state's eight regions perform in an open-air auditorium on the Cerro del Fortín and, at the end of each dance, throw fruit, bread and other gifts to the audience. The main days are the Lunes del Cerro, usually the two Mondays after 16 July (in 2026, 20 and 27 July), but check the official program each year: the dates can move.

Elsewhere: In the Southern Hemisphere the long summer vacation is in January and February: Argentines and Chileans go to the beach while Mexico is in winter.

Sources

  • Ley Federal del Trabajo, art. 74 (rest days; Monday holidays since the reform published in the Diario Oficial de la Federación, 17 January 2006), art. 76 (12 days of vacation, reform in force since 1 January 2023) and art. 87 (aguinaldo).
  • Secretaría de Educación Pública (SEP), Calendario escolar 2025–2026.
  • Gobierno del Estado de Oaxaca, Secretaría de Turismo: Guelaguetza official program (Lunes del Cerro).

Role play: Planning a long weekend

You and Mariana have a long weekend coming up. Plan it together: where you'll go, when you'll leave and come back, what you'll do, and a plan B if the weather is bad. Use tú.

You

  1. Say where you're planning to go (pienso ir a…).
  2. Say when you'll leave and come back (saldremos, regresaremos).
  3. Suggest two activities in the future (visitaremos, comeremos…).
  4. Make a plan B with si + present, future.
  5. Promise to do one thing (haré, compraré…).

Mariana

  1. Asks what you're planning to do over the long weekend.
  2. Asks when you'll leave.
  3. Asks what you'll do there.
  4. Asks: what if it rains?
  5. Offers to do one thing and asks what you'll do.
