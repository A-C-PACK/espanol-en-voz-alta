Fíjate Notice

Read how it works. Play the examples and repeat them aloud.

Saying sorry: perdón, perdóname, lo siento, disculpa

Spanish has several ways to say "sorry", and they're not all the same. Perdón is quick: a small mistake, or bumping into someone. Perdóname (tú) / perdóneme (usted) is a real apology: "forgive me". Lo siento (mucho) means "I'm (very) sorry": for bad news, or when you can't do something. Disculpa (tú) / disculpe (usted) is "excuse me" to get someone's attention, or sorry for a small thing.

Spanishwhen
¡Perdón!a quick sorry; excuse me
Perdóname. / Perdóneme.a real apology (tú / usted)
Lo siento (mucho).I'm (very) sorry: regret, bad news, saying no
Disculpa. / Disculpe.excuse me; sorry (tú / usted)

From the conversation:

  • ¡Perdón, Alex! Ya estoy llegando. Estoy saliendo del metro.Sorry, Alex! I'm almost there. I'm just coming out of the metro.
  • ¡Hola a todos! Sofía, ¡feliz cumpleaños! Perdóname, de veras. Es que el metro va lentísimo.Hi, everyone! Sofía, happy birthday! I'm really sorry. The thing is, the metro is incredibly slow.
  • Lo siento mucho, Diego, pero ese día es la boda de mi prima en Toluca. ¡Pero muchas gracias!I'm really sorry, Diego, but that day is my cousin's wedding in Toluca. But thank you so much!

To say you're late, use the present: Lo siento, llego tarde (sorry I'm late). Don't say estoy tarde.

Giving a reason: es que…, and accepting an apology

After an apology, Mexicans almost always give a reason, and it starts with es que… ("the thing is…"): Es que hay mucho tráfico. It softens any apology or refusal. To accept an apology, say No te preocupes (tú) / No se preocupe (usted) (don't worry), No pasa nada (it's fine) or No hay problema.

From the conversation:

  • No te preocupes. Aquí te estamos esperando.Don't worry. We're waiting for you here.
  • ¡Ay, Diego, no pasa nada! Qué bueno que estás aquí. Siéntate.Oh, Diego, it's fine! I'm so glad you're here. Sit down.
  • Perdón, Rocío. Lo siento, llego tarde. Es que hay mucho tráfico en Eje Central.Sorry, Rocío. I'm sorry I'm late. The thing is, there's a lot of traffic on Eje Central.

Invitations: me gustaría + infinitivo

me gustaría · te gustaría · le gustaría + infinitivo

To invite: ¿Quieres venir?, Te invito a…, Estás invitado / invitada. To answer, me gustaría (I would like) is the polite key phrase, an Andamio structure: learn it as a chunk + infinitive. Yes: ¡Claro que sí! Me gustaría mucho ir. No: Me gustaría mucho, pero… + a reason, or Lo siento mucho, pero… Thank the person either way. A ver si se puede (we'll see if I can) sounds positive, but it's often a soft no.

From the conversation:

  • ¿En serio? ¡Claro que sí! Me gustaría mucho ir. ¿Tengo que llevar algo?Really? Of course! I'd love to go. Do I have to bring anything?
  • ¡Ay, gracias! Me gustaría mucho, pero ese fin de semana voy a estar trabajando en un proyecto muy grande. A ver si se puede.Oh, thank you! I'd love to, but that weekend I'm going to be working on a really big project. We'll see if I can.
  • ¡Qué padre! Me gustaría mucho, pero el sábado voy a estar en Oaxaca. Lo siento mucho.How cool! I'd love to, but on Saturday I'm going to be in Oaxaca. I'm really sorry.

Right now: estar + gerundio

estoy · estás · está · estamos · están + -ando / -iendo

Estar + a gerund (the -ing form) describes something in progress: right now (¿Qué estás haciendo?) or these days (estoy trabajando en un proyecto). Another Andamio structure. To make the gerund: -ar → -ando (trabajar → trabajando), -er / -ir → -iendo (hacer → haciendo, salir → saliendo). The simple present can also mean "now" (4.2), but estar + gerundio stresses that it's happening at this moment.

infinitivegerundexample
esperaresperandoTe estamos esperando.
prepararpreparandoEstá preparando su fiesta.
hacerhaciendo¿Qué estás haciendo?
salirsaliendoEstoy saliendo del metro.
leerleyendoEstoy leyendo.
dormirdurmiendoDiego está durmiendo.

From the conversation:

  • Ahorita le llamo… Diego, ¿dónde estás? ¿Qué estás haciendo?I'll call him right now… Diego, where are you? What are you doing?
  • Muy emocionada. Está preparando su fiesta de quince años: es el sábado veintiocho de noviembre, en Puebla. Alex, mis papás te invitan.Very excited. She's getting her fifteenth-birthday party ready: it's on Saturday the twenty-eighth of November, in Puebla. Alex, my parents are inviting you.
  • ¡Alex! Ya estamos empezando la junta.Alex! We're just starting the meeting.

Two irregular gerunds to learn now: leer → leyendo, dormir → durmiendo. And Mexicans say ya estoy llegando for "I'm almost there".

Cultura Culture

Read about the culture behind the language.

Yes, no, maybe: invitations in Mexico

In Mexico a flat no can sound rude, so people often decline indirectly: me gustaría mucho, pero…, a ver si se puede (we'll see), ahí te aviso (I'll let you know), or even an enthusiastic ¡claro que sí! that may really mean "maybe". Context tells you: if there's no time, no detail and no follow-up, it's probably a no. Studies of Mexican Spanish show that speakers usually soften a refusal with thanks, a reason (es que…) and an offer for another time (otro día). Do the same, and don't push for a direct answer.

Los quince años (also la quinceañera or los XV) is a big celebration of a girl's fifteenth birthday: often a thanksgiving Mass, then a party with dinner, a vals (waltz) with her chambelanes (escorts), family padrinos who help pay for the cake, the dress or the music, and dancing until late. Some families now choose a trip instead of a party.

When you're invited to someone's home, don't arrive empty-handed: ask ¿Qué llevo? and bring something even if they say nada: a dessert, a bottle of wine, drinks or tortillas. For a birthday, a small present (un detalle) is enough. And remember 8.1: for a party at home, arriving a little after the time is normal.

Elsewhere: The quinceañera tradition is also strong in Central America, the Caribbean (Cuba, Puerto Rico, the Dominican Republic) and among Latinos in the US; in Argentina and Uruguay, people say la fiesta de quince. In Spain it is not a tradition.

Sources

  • J. César Félix-Brasdefer (2008), Politeness in Mexico and the United States: A contrastive study of the realization and perception of refusals, John Benjamins.
  • Instituto Cervantes (2006), Plan curricular, «Saberes y comportamientos socioculturales: celebraciones y ceremonias; relaciones sociales (invitaciones, visitas)».

Role play: Late for dinner

You arrive 40 minutes late to a dinner with Mariana at a restaurant (it's not a party: you agreed on a time). Apologize and explain. Then she invites you to something; say no politely and suggest another day. Use tú.

You

  1. Greet her and apologize (perdóname / lo siento).
  2. Give a reason with es que…
  3. Say what you're doing these days (estoy + gerundio).
  4. Answer her invitation: me gustaría mucho, pero… + a reason.
  5. Suggest another day.

Mariana

  1. Says she's been waiting (a little annoyed, then friendly).
  2. Accepts the apology: no te preocupes.
  3. Asks what you're doing these days.
  4. Invites you to her sister Lucía's party on Saturday.
  5. Accepts your new idea.
