Fíjate Notice

Read how it works. Play the examples and repeat them aloud.

Numbers 0–100

Learn 0–15 by heart. From 16 to 29 the numbers join into one word: dieciséis ("ten and six"), veintiuno, veintidós… From 31 on, they are separate words with y: treinta y uno, cuarenta y cinco. 100 is cien.

Numbers from 0 to 100
0 cero1 uno2 dos3 tres
4 cuatro5 cinco6 seis7 siete
8 ocho9 nueve10 diez11 once
12 doce13 trece14 catorce15 quince
16 dieciséis17 diecisiete18 dieciocho19 diecinueve
20 veinte21 veintiuno22 veintidós28 veintiocho
30 treinta32 treinta y dos40 cuarenta50 cincuenta
60 sesenta70 setenta80 ochenta90 noventa

From the conversation:

  • cero, uno, dos, tres, cuatro, cinco, seis, siete, ocho, nueve, diez0–10
  • once, doce, trece, catorce, quince, dieciséis, diecisiete, dieciocho, diecinueve, veinte11–20
  • treinta, cuarenta, cincuenta, sesenta, setenta, ochenta, noventa, cien30–100
  • Tengo treinta y dos años.I'm thirty-two.

Careful with these pairs: seis (6) / siete (7); sesenta (60) / setenta (70); doce (12) / dos (2). And before a noun, uno becomes un: veintiún años, treinta y un años, setenta y un años (with an accent on veintiún).

Age: tener

tengo · tienes · tiene + número + años

Spanish uses tener (to have) for age: you have years. ¿Cuántos años tienes? (tú) / ¿Cuántos años tiene? (usted). Answer Tengo 32 años; don't use soy for age.

From the conversation:

  • ¿Y cuántos años tiene?And how old are you?
  • Tengo treinta y dos años.I'm thirty-two.

¿Cuál es tu / su…?

To ask for a detail, say ¿Cuál es tu…? (tú) or ¿Cuál es su…? (usted): nombre (name), apellido (last name), número de celular, correo (email), dirección (address). In email addresses, @ is arroba, a dot is punto, and _ is guion bajo. Phone numbers are said in pairs: cincuenta y cinco, veintiocho…

From the conversation:

  • Muy bien. ¿Cuál es su número de celular?Very good. What's your cell phone number?
  • Alex punto Morgan arroba correo punto com.alex dot morgan at correo dot com.

Rocío says su because she uses usted; after háblame de tú, Alex would hear tu: ¿Cuál es tu correo?

Cultura Culture

Read about the culture behind the language.

Two last names, ten digits and a CURP

Most people in Mexico have two last names: first the father's (apellido paterno), then the mother's (apellido materno). Ana Sofía Herrera Domínguez is usually just "Ana Sofía Herrera" in conversation, but official forms ask for both. When a woman marries, she legally keeps her own last names.

Phone numbers in Mexico have ten digits, and since 2019 you always dial all ten, even locally. The first two or three are the area: 55 for Mexico City, 33 for Guadalajara, 81 for Monterrey. People usually say them in pairs: cincuenta y cinco, veintiocho, catorce…

On many official forms you'll also see CURP (Clave Única de Registro de Población), an 18-character personal ID code that every resident has. Foreign residents get one too, with their residence card.

Elsewhere: Two last names are the norm in most Spanish-speaking countries; in Argentina, people usually have just one. In Spain, parents have been able to agree on the order of the two last names since 1999, and since 2017 the father's name no longer goes first by default.

Sources

  • Instituto Federal de Telecomunicaciones (IFT): marcación a 10 dígitos, in force from 3 August 2019.
  • Registro Nacional de Población (RENAPO), ¿Qué es la CURP?
  • Spain: Ley 40/1999 (order of last names by agreement) and Ley 20/2011 del Registro Civil, art. 49 (in force 30 June 2017). Argentina: Código Civil y Comercial (2015), art. 64.

Role play: Signing up at a gym

You want to join a gym near your house in Mexico City. The receptionist fills out a form with your details. She uses usted. Use your own details.

You

  1. Greet her and say you'd like to sign up (Quiero inscribirme, "I want to sign up").
  2. Give your full name and spell your last name.
  3. Say how old you are.
  4. Give your cell phone number, in pairs.
  5. Give your email address; say thank you and goodbye.

La recepcionista

  1. Greets you and asks your name.
  2. Asks how to spell your last name.
  3. Asks your age.
  4. Asks for your cell phone number and repeats it.
  5. Asks for your email; says listo and goodbye.
